 k^2 \tilde{\phi}_{\bar{k}} (p) = \frac{\displaystyle 4\pi \sum_{\alpha} q_{\alpha} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{ f_{\alpha \bar{k}} (v,t=0) }{p+i\bar{k}\cdot \bar{v}} \, d^3v }{\displaystyle 1 - \frac{4\pi}{k^2} i \sum_{\alpha} \frac{q_{\alpha}^2}{m_{\alpha}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}  \frac{\bar{k}\cdot \nabla_{\bar{v}} f_{\alpha 0}}{p+i\bar{k} \cdot \bar{v}} \, d^3v }
$$
we want to antitransform this 
$$
 \phi_{k} (t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{p_{0}-i\infty}^{p_{0}+i \infty} \exp(pt) \tilde{\phi}_{k}(p) \,dp
$$
We rotate the reference frame such that one of the new axis is parallel to $\bar{k}$ 
The new reference frame will be ($\hat{u}, \hat{w}_{1}, \hat{w}_{2}$) with $\hat{u} \parallel \bar{k}$ 
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 12-35-35.png|190]]
$$
 u = \frac{\bar{k}\cdot \bar{v}}{k} \implies \bar{k} \cdot \bar{v} = ku \implies \bar{k} \cdot \nabla _{\bar{v}} = k \frac{ \partial  }{ \partial u } 
$$
so 
$$
 k^2 \tilde{\phi}_{\bar{k}} (p) = \frac{\displaystyle 4\pi \sum_{\alpha} q_{\alpha} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{ f_{\alpha \bar{k}} (u,w_{1},w_{2}) }{p+iku} \, du \, dw_{1} \, dw_{2} }{\displaystyle 1 - \frac{4\pi}{k^\cancel{ 2 }} i \sum_{\alpha} \frac{q_{\alpha}^2}{m_{\alpha}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}  \cancel{ k } \frac{ \partial f_{\alpha 0} (u,w_{1},w_{2}) / \partial u  }{p+iku} \, du \, dw_{1} \, dw_{2} }
$$
The poles are only along $u$ since
$$
p = -iku \implies u = \frac{ip}{k}
$$
We define (at $t=0$)
$$
\begin{align}
F_{\alpha 0} (u) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{\alpha 0} (u,w_{1},w_{2}) \, dw_{1} \, dw_{2} \\[.8em]
F_{\alpha \bar{k}} (u) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{\alpha \bar{k}} (u,w_{1},w_{2}) \, dw_{1} \, dw_{2}
\end{align}
$$
(we assume $f$ as having all the properties for the integral to be stable).
We substitute into $\phi$ and
$$
\begin{align}
 k^2 \tilde{\phi}_{\bar{k}} (p) &= \frac{\displaystyle 4\pi \sum_{\alpha} q_{\alpha} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{ F_{\alpha \bar{k}} (u) }{p+iku} \, du }{\displaystyle 1 - \frac{4\pi}{k} i \sum_{\alpha} \frac{q_{\alpha}^2}{m_{\alpha}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}   \frac{ \partial F_{\alpha 0} (u) / \partial u  }{p+iku} \, du } = \\[1em]
&= \frac{\displaystyle (-i)\frac{4\pi}{k} \sum_{\alpha} q_{\alpha} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{ F_{\alpha \bar{k}} (u) }{u - ip / k} \, du }{\displaystyle 1 - \frac{4\pi}{k^2} \cancel{ i } \sum_{\alpha} \frac{q_{\alpha}^2}{m_{\alpha}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}   \frac{ \partial F_{\alpha 0} (u) / \partial u  }{u - ip / k} \, du }
\end{align}
$$
where in the last passage we collected $ik$ in both the integrals
The denominator is the **Electrostatic Dielectric Function** 
$$
D(k, ip) \equiv 1 - \sum_{\alpha} \frac{\omega_{p \alpha}^2}{k^2 n_{\alpha 0}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{ \partial F_{\alpha 0} (u) / \partial u  }{u - ip / k} \, du
$$
and
$$
 \phi_{k} (t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{p_{0}-i\infty}^{p_{0}+i \infty} \frac{ (-i)\frac{4\pi}{k} \sum_{\alpha} q_{\alpha} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{ F_{\alpha \bar{k}} (u,t=0) }{u - ip / k} \, du }{ D(k, ip) } \exp(pt) \,dp
$$
for $\mathrm{Re} \{p\} \geq p_{0}$ (from the def of Laplace transform)
Now we find the poles of $D(k, ip) = 0$  
PIC - poles on the left of p0
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 13-05-18.png|300]]
They are all on the left of $p_{0}$, because on the right of $p_{0}$ we have the validity of the Laplace Transform.

To use the residue theorem, we need to embrace al the poles, so we need to modify the domain of $S$. We need to use the analytical continuation of the $f$. 
PIC - residue theorem
![[IMG_20251017_102550505_HDR~2.jpg|600]]
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 12-36-05.png|200]]
So we need the analytical continuation of $\hat{\phi}_{\bar{k}} (p)$, we call it 
$$
\hat{\Phi}_{\bar{k}} (p) = \begin{cases}
\hat{\phi}_{\bar{k}} (p) &&& \mathrm{Re} \{p\} \geq p_{0} \\[.6em]
\text{Analitical continuation of }\hat{\phi}_{\bar{k}} (p) &&& \mathrm{Re} \{p\} < p_{0} 
\end{cases}
$$
We use the Landau prescription.
Both integrals can be written as 
$$
\int \frac{g(u)}{u - ip / k} du, \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{with } g(u) = \begin{cases}
F_{\alpha 0}(u,t=0) &&\text{for the numerator}\\[.6em]
 \partial F_{\alpha 0} / \partial u  &&\text{for the denominator}
\end{cases}
$$
PIC - how to avoid poles in integration
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 12-36-29.png|400]]
For the first case, $\mathrm{Re} \{p\} \geq 0$ and we don't have any problems.

For the second case, $\mathrm{Re} \{p\} = 0$ and we need to modify the integration path.
We use
$$
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{g(u)}{u - ip / k} \, du = P \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}  \frac{g(u)}{u - ip / k} \, du + \pi i \cdot g \left( \frac{ip}{k} \right)
$$
and 
$$
P \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}  \frac{g(u)}{u - ip / k} \, du  = \lim_{ \varepsilon \to 0 } \left[  \int_{-\infty}^{ip/k -\varepsilon} + \int^{\infty}_{ip/k +\varepsilon} \right] \frac{g(u)}{u - ip / k} \, du
$$

For the third case, $\mathrm{Re} \{p\} < 0$ 
PIC - 3rd case
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 13-06-40.png|400]]
In order to keep $\Phi$ analytic, we also have to move the integration path.
We use 
$$
 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{g(u)}{u - ip / k} \, du = P \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}  \frac{g(u)}{u - ip / k} \, du + 2 \pi i \cdot g \left( \frac{ip}{k} \right)
$$
(a compact way to write this is $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \to \int _L$).  

So we have
$$
 \phi_{k} (t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{p_{0}-i\infty}^{p_{0}+i \infty} \frac{ (-i)\frac{4\pi}{k} \sum_{\alpha} q_{\alpha} \int_{L} \frac{ F_{\alpha \bar{k}} (u,t=0) }{u - ip / k} \, du }{ D(k, ip) } \exp(pt) \,dp
$$
for $\mathrm{Re} \{p\} \geq p_{0}$ 
and
$$
D(k, ip) \equiv 1 - \sum_{\alpha} \frac{\omega_{p \alpha}^2}{k^2 n_{\alpha 0}} \int_{L} \frac{ \partial F_{\alpha 0} (u) / \partial u  }{u - ip / k} \, du
$$
Now we follow the explanation by the book.
PIC - book approach vs landau approach
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 12-38-29.png]]
(Landau method breaks in the non-analiticy zone and the mathematical structure needs to be corrected)
In the book method we need
$$
k^2 \phi_{\bar{k}} (t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \left[ \int_{p_{0}-i\infty}^{p_{0}-i\beta} + \int^{-\alpha-i\beta}_{p_{0}-i\beta} + \int^{-\alpha+i\beta}_{-\alpha-i\beta} + \int_{-\alpha+i\beta}^{p_{0}+i\beta} + \int^{p_{0}+i\infty}_{p_{0}+i\beta} \right]  ( \cdots ) \,dp + \text{poles}
$$
Assumptions:
1) $\beta \to \infty$
	then only $\int^{-\alpha+i\beta}_{-\alpha-i\beta} ( \cdots ) \,dp$ survives because for all the others $D(k, ip) \to \infty$ and they all tend to zero
2) long time limit (asymptotic)  
	we neglect the short transient of perturbations and we focus on the long-term behaviour of the system.
	We know an upper limit for the remaining integral $\int^{-\alpha+i\beta}_{-\alpha-i\beta} ( \cdots ) \,dp \sim \exp (-At^2)$ and we can also neglect it.

We are left with the contribution of the poles, which is 
$$
\sum_{j} R_{j} \exp [p_{j} (k) t]
$$
with 
- $p_{j}(k)$ roots of $D(k,ip)$  
- $\displaystyle R_{j} = \lim_{ p \to p_{j} } (p-p_{j}) \, \tilde{\Phi}_{\bar{k}}(p)$  
and 
$$
 k^2 \phi_{\bar{k}}(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \left\{ \cancel{ \int^{-\alpha+i\beta}_{-\alpha-i\beta} ( \cdots ) \,dp } + 2\pi i \sum_{j} R_{j} \exp[p_{j}(k) t] \right\} \approx \sum_{j} R_{j} \exp[p_{j}(k) t]
$$
Now using 
$$
\begin{align}
i p_{j}(k) = \omega_{j}(k) &&\implies&& p_{j}(k) = -i \omega_{j}(k)
\end{align}
$$
we derive 
$$
\begin{align}
D(k,\omega) = 0 &&\implies&& \omega = \omega (k), \ \ \ \ \omega \in \mathbb{C}
\end{align}
$$
So 
$$
\begin{align}
 \phi_{k}(t) &\simeq \sum_{j} R_{j} \exp[-i \omega_{j}(k) t] = \\[.8em]
&=  \sum_{j} R_{j} \exp[-i (\omega_{Rj}(k) + i \omega_{Ij}(k)) t] =  \\[.8em]
&= \sum_{j} R_{j} \exp[-i \omega_{Rj}(k)t] \exp [\omega_{Ij}(k)) t]
\end{align}
$$
where the last exponential (with the $\omega_{I j}$ term) is responsible for the amplification or the damping of the wave. This factor is not predicted at all in fluid theory. 

For $f_{\alpha 0}$ as a Maxwellian distribution at equilibrium
$$
 \omega_{I j} < 0 \implies \text{Landau damping}
$$
PIC - landau damping
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 12-38-44.png|400]]
We have the energy paradox.
The system is isotropic so the energy is conserved and we could invert time. 
Landau damping disperse energy, where does the energy go? If we reverse time, where would the energy come from?

Could, Malburg and O'Neil solved this paradox and showed where the energy goes using the **Plasma Echo Theory**.

We solve for $\omega$ 
$$
 D(k,\omega) = 1- \sum_{\alpha} \frac{\omega_{p, \alpha}^2}{k^2 n_{\alpha 0}} \int_{L} \frac{\partial F_{\alpha 0} (u) / \partial u}{u - \omega /k} \, du = 0
$$
We will see that 
$$
D(k, \omega_{R} + i \omega_{I} ) \approx D(k,\omega_{R}) + i \omega_{I} \frac{ \partial D(k,\omega_{R}) }{ \partial \omega_{R} } 
$$
in fact , we assume
$$
|\omega_{I} / \omega_{R}| \ll 1
$$
We see $|\omega_{I} / \omega_{R}| \ll 1$ (in picture A) compared to $|\omega_{I} / \omega_{R}| \gg 1$ (in picture B) 
![[Screenshot from 2025-11-18 12-39-01.png|400]]
We are interested in $\omega_{I} < 0$ because we cannot even observe a wave otherwise.
For $\omega_{I} > 0$ the linear approximation is not valid anymore and the wave grows in time exponentially.

We Taylor expand this in $\omega_{I} = 0$ and, using the Landau prescription (the poles are on the real axis)
$$
\begin{align}
D(k, \omega_{R}) &= 1- \sum_{\alpha} \frac{\omega_{p, \alpha}^2}{k^2 n_{\alpha 0}} \int_{L} \frac{\partial F_{\alpha 0} (u) / \partial u}{u - \omega_{R} /k} \, du \approx \\[.8em]
& \approx \underbrace{ 1- \sum_{\alpha} \frac{\omega_{p, \alpha}^2}{k^2 n_{\alpha 0}} \cdot P \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\partial F_{\alpha 0} (u) / \partial u}{u - \omega_{R} /k} \, du }_{ D_{R}(k,\omega_{R}) } - \underbrace{ \sum_{\alpha} \frac{\omega_{p, \alpha}^2}{k^2 n_{\alpha 0}} \cdot i \pi \left. \frac{ \partial F_{\alpha 0}(u) }{ \partial u }  \right|_{\omega_{R} / k} }_{ D_{I}(k,\omega_{R}) }
\end{align}
$$
and
$$
 D(k, \omega_{R} +  i \omega_{I}) \approx \left[ D_{R}(k, \omega_{R}) - \omega_{I} \frac{ \partial D_{I}(k, \omega_{R}) }{ \partial \omega_{R} }  \right]  + i \left[ D_{I}(k, \omega_{R}) + \omega_{I} \frac{ \partial D_{R}(k, \omega_{R}) }{ \partial \omega_{R} }  \right] 
$$
To find the zeros we need 
$$
\begin{cases}
 D_{R}(k, \omega_{R}) - \omega_{I} \dfrac{ \partial D_{I}(k, \omega_{R}) }{ \partial \omega_{R} }  = 0 \\[.8em]
D_{I}(k, \omega_{R}) + \omega_{I} \dfrac{ \partial D_{R}(k, \omega_{R}) }{ \partial \omega_{R} } =0
\end{cases}
$$
So in the first equation we neglect the second term since
$$
 \omega_{I} \dfrac{ \partial D_{I}(k, \omega_{R}) }{ \partial \omega_{R} } \sim \frac{\omega_{I}}{\omega_{R}} \implies D_{R}(k, \omega_{R}) = 0
$$
from wich we can obtain the dispersion relation
$$
\omega_{R} = \omega_{R}(k)
$$
In the second equation, we keep the second term and we get
$$
\omega_{I} = -\frac{D_{I}}{ \partial D_{R}   / \partial \omega _{R}  }
$$
otherwise we lose the Landau Damping effect.
